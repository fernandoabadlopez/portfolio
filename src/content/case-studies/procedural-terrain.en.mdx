## Context

Procedural terrain generation builds a landscape from an algorithm instead of sculpting it by hand. A heightmap is filled by a subdivision or noise function, reshaped by simulated erosion, and then dressed with objects and materials. Changing a seed or a parameter produces a new terrain in seconds, which is why these techniques sit under most open-world and simulation pipelines.

This project covers that whole chain inside Unity: a custom editor tool that generates, erodes and populates terrains, and a set of shaders that render them with triplanar texturing, snow that reacts to the scene, and animated water. Everything runs in a playable scene where the terrain can be explored and hit.

## Overview

The project is organised as a pipeline of five stages:

1. **Generate** a heightmap with Midpoint Displacement, Diamond-Square or fractal Brownian motion.
2. **Erode** it with thermal erosion.
3. **Populate** it with object instances.
4. **Shade** it with triplanar mapping, orientation-based snow and a water surface.
5. **Interact** with it from a first-person controller that changes the amount of snow and fires projectiles at it.

Generation, erosion and spawning live in one editor tool in the Unity inspector. The terrain shaders are hand-written HLSL inside Custom Function nodes (`TriplanarMapping`, `TriplanarSnowBlend`, `SnowVertexDisplacement`) wired together in Shader Graph, while the water is built entirely from Shader Graph nodes.

## The terrain editor tool

All generation happens from a single inspector panel. A global block sets the parameters shared by the subdivision algorithms: the number of divisions `N`, the noise amplitude `A`, the amplitude falloff `H` and a random seed. Below it, each technique has its own section with a target `Terrain` and a button that regenerates it, so the algorithms can be compared side by side on separate terrains.

## Midpoint Displacement and Diamond-Square

Both algorithms build the heightmap by recursive subdivision. They start from the corners of the grid, fill in new points from the average of their neighbours plus a random offset, and repeat on ever smaller cells. `N` sets how many subdivision levels run, `A` sets the size of the first offsets, and `H` reduces that amplitude at every level, which decides how rough or smooth the result looks.

Midpoint Displacement fills the midpoints along the edges of each cell. Diamond-Square alternates two steps: the diamond step sets the centre of each square from its four corners, and the square step sets the edge midpoints from the surrounding diamond. That alternation spreads the random offsets more evenly and reduces the axis-aligned creases that plain midpoint subdivision tends to leave.

## Fractal Brownian motion and hybrid multifractal

Fractal Brownian motion sums several layers, or octaves, of noise. Each octave multiplies the frequency by the frequency delta and the amplitude by the amplitude delta, so every layer adds finer and fainter detail. The capture uses 8 octaves, a base amplitude of 0.5 and frequency of 1, an amplitude delta (gain) of 0.5 and a frequency delta of 2.

With **Hybrid Multifractal** enabled, each octave is weighted by the value of the ones before it. Low, smooth areas stay smooth and detail concentrates on higher ground, which gives valleys and ridges a different character instead of a uniformly noisy surface.

## Thermal erosion

Thermal erosion simulates material sliding down slopes that are too steep to hold it. For each cell, the height difference with its lower neighbours is compared to a threshold `T`; where it is exceeded, a fraction `c` of the excess moves downhill. Repeating the pass softens sharp peaks and piles material at the foot of slopes. The capture uses `c = 0.5` and `T = 0.01`, and the tool reports the minimum and maximum height of the terrain after erosion (0.0108 and 1.0).

## Triplanar mapping

Triplanar mapping is a texturing technique that solves the classic problem of applying textures to terrains with steep slopes. With normal UVs, the texture stretches on vertical areas because the coordinates are meant for horizontal surfaces. Triplanar projects the texture from three orthogonal directions (X, Y, Z) and blends the results based on the surface normal, eliminating stretching regardless of orientation. It's the foundation of almost any modern game terrain with cliffs or non-flat geometry.

The triplanar shader samples the same texture three times, once per each orthogonal plane (XY, XZ, YZ), using world coordinates as UVs. It then blends the three samples using the surface normal as weight: the axis whose normal component is largest dominates the result.

On a horizontal surface (normal pointing up), the XZ projection dominates and you see the texture from the Y axis. On a vertical cliff (normal pointing sideways), the lateral projection wins and the texture is projected correctly, without stretching. The transition between projections is continuous because the blending uses the squared components of the normal, which gives a smooth weight with no discontinuities.

## Snow accumulation by surface orientation

Snow doesn't cover the entire terrain uniformly. In reality it accumulates on horizontal surfaces and barely appears on vertical ones. The shader replicates this by using the Y component of the normal as a mask: surfaces with a normal pointing upward receive snow, sideways surfaces don't.

The result is that snow appears on peaks and flatlands, respects cliffs by leaving them with their original rock texture, and creates natural transitions on intermediate slopes. No extra geometry, no manually painted masks.

## Real-time control from the player

The shader exposes a `_SnowStrenght` parameter that controls how much snow there is in the scene. The `FPSController` in C# adjusts it with the keys `X` (more snow) and `Z` (less), and the change is reflected immediately on the terrain material. `_MaxThickness` is also adjusted, controlling the physical vertex displacement, so raising snow amount doesn't just change color — the actual geometry grows upward.

This is implemented in `SnowVertexDisplacement`, a shader that displaces the terrain mesh vertices upward according to the `_MaxThickness` value, attenuated by the same surface orientation mask. Snow gains physical volume, not just a texture change.

## Reaction to physical impacts

The most specific part of the system is local deformation on impact. When the player fires a ball with left click (`FPSController.Shoot()`), the ball carries a `BulletBounce` component that detects collision against the terrain. On impact:

1. The exact collision point is obtained with `collision.contacts[0].point`.
2. The point height is adjusted by adding the current `_MaxThickness`, because snow has volume and the physical collision point sits at the base ground, not at the visible snow surface.
3. Four parameters are passed to the shader: `_HitPos` (impact position), `_HitTime` (impact time), `_BounceRadius` (affected area radius), and `_BounceStrength` (bounce intensity).

`SnowVertexDisplacement` uses these parameters to modify the local displacement with an elastic bounce: vertices within the impact radius sink and rise back in a damped oscillation, leaving no permanent crater. It's the same effect you see in Fortnite when you shoot at a structure: a brief, visible deformation that recovers. Simplified but effective, without real physics simulation.

The detail of adding `_MaxThickness` to the impact point Y is what makes the deformation feel visually correct. Without that adjustment, with thick snow enabled, bounces would happen below the visible surface.

## Water shader

The water surface is built in Shader Graph, with its motion in the vertex stage and its pattern in the fragment stage.

In the vertex stage, the object-space position is split and its X and Z components are offset by time multiplied by `WaveSpeed`. That scrolling coordinate feeds a Simple Noise node scaled by `NoiseScale`; the noise is multiplied by `WaveStrength`, offset by `Height`, and written back as the vertex Y. The surface rises and falls in slowly travelling waves.

In the fragment stage, a Voronoi node tiled 10 by 10, with a cell density of 3 and its angle offset driven by time, produces cells that shift continuously. Raising the result to the power of 5 keeps only the brightest cell borders, and adding them to a blue base colour draws bright, moving lines across the water. Smoothness is set to 0.5 and transparency comes from an `Opacity` property.
